The easiest trap in engineering is becoming the person everyone needs for the same question.

At first, it feels like value.

You know the system. You know the weird setting. You know which script has to run before the other script. You know the one portal blade that lies unless you refresh it twice and pretend you meant to do that.

That kind of knowledge matters.

But if the team cannot move without you, eventually you cannot move either.

The goal should not be to become indispensable.

The goal should be to become unnecessary in yesterday's work so you are available for tomorrow's problems.

## Indispensable Is A Bottleneck

Being indispensable sounds good until you look at the operational shape of it.

Every repeat question routes through you.

Every fragile process waits for you.

Every outage has your name floating nearby because nobody else knows where the bodies are buried.

That is not leverage. That is a human dependency.

Sometimes that happens because the work is complicated. Sometimes it happens because the work was never cleaned up after it started working. Most internal tools, scripts, and processes have this problem. They are built under pressure, validated by one person, then quietly promoted into "production" because they solved the immediate pain.

Then six months later, everyone is afraid to touch them.

That is why making yourself obsolete matters.

Not because your knowledge is worthless, but because your knowledge should leave a shape behind.

## Repeated Work Is A Signal

The first time you solve a problem, you are learning.

The second time, you are confirming the pattern.

The third time, you should be suspicious.

Repeated work is usually telling you one of four things:

- The process should be automated.
- The decision should be documented.
- The ownership is unclear.
- The system is too fragile.

None of those get fixed by being the hero again.

Heroics feel good in the moment. They also hide the actual defect. If the same person has to rescue the same workflow every month, the workflow is still broken.

The fix is usually boring.

Write the runbook. Add the logging. Turn the manual click path into a script. Put the weird caveat next to the command that triggers it. Teach someone else how to recover it without you.

It is not glamorous.

It works.

## Leave Work Behind You

Good engineering should make the system easier to operate after you touch it.

That does not always mean writing code. Sometimes the highest-value change is a short note with the exact failure mode and the exact recovery steps. Sometimes it is a checklist. Sometimes it is deleting an option nobody should have been using.

The point is to reduce the amount of private memory required to keep things moving.

Private memory does not scale.

It also does not survive vacations, promotions, team changes, layoffs, sick days, burnout, or the very human act of forgetting what you were thinking when you wrote that thing at 11:47 PM.

If a process depends on your memory, the process is not done.

If a script only makes sense while you are narrating it, the script is not done.

If a decision has to be re-litigated every time a new person joins the project, the decision is not done.

Done means someone else can operate it safely.

## Teach The Exit Path

There is a specific kind of career growth that comes from refusing to hoard context.

You explain the work.

Then you document the work.

Then you let someone else do the work.

Then you stay close enough to help without grabbing the keyboard back.

That last part is hard.

It is faster to do it yourself. It is cleaner to do it yourself. It is emotionally satisfying to be the person who knows the answer.

It is also how you stay stuck.

Teaching creates capacity. Documentation creates capacity. Automation creates capacity. Every time you remove yourself from old work, you create room to take on a harder class of problem.

That is the real career advantage.

Not knowing the newest tool before everyone else.

Knowing how to turn hard-won experience into something other people can reuse.

## Make Tomorrow Possible

I still care about learning new technology.

You have to. The industry keeps moving whether you are ready or not.

But the newest tool is rarely the advantage by itself. The advantage is what you do with what you learn.

Can you turn it into a repeatable pattern?

Can you make the failure modes visible?

Can you explain the tradeoffs without sounding like a product page?

Can you leave the next person with something better than "ask Jorge"?

That is the part I keep trying to practice.

I want the work to get clearer after I touch it.

I want the system to need me less.

I want the team to know more.

And I want to be free to go be confused by a newer, harder problem.

## Operating Rules

The rules I try to follow are simple:

- If I explain it twice, write it down.
- If I do it three times, automate it or make it boring.
- If only I can operate it, I have not finished the job.
- If the output is unclear, future me will pay for it.
- If the process depends on memory, the process is broken.
- If the handoff requires a meeting, the documentation is not done.

I do not always live up to this.

Nobody does.

But it is the direction I try to move in: automate the repeated parts, document the sharp edges, teach the pattern, and move on to the next challenge.

Make yourself unnecessary in yesterday's work.

That is how you stay available for tomorrow's opportunities.
