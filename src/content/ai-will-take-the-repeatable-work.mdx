A lot of IT work is repetitive.

Reset the password. Add the user to a group. Run the same script again. Follow the screenshots in the wiki.

AI does not have to replace IT to remove IT jobs. It only has to make the repetitive work cheap enough that companies need fewer people to do it.

If most of your week is known requests with known fixes, your job title will not protect you.

Accountability is not job security. Someone still has to own the outcome when the automation gets it wrong. That might be one senior engineer, a manager, an MSP, or a vendor. The company can keep the accountability and cut the team.

## Why Companies Will Do It

Companies notice cost.

One person using AI can clear a queue that used to need several people. Password resets and group changes are obvious examples. Help desk and desktop support have plenty more. Identity, cloud, network, and security teams have their own versions.

The pattern is simple: the request is known, the fix is known, and the result is easy to check. AI does not have to be brilliant to handle that work. It has to be fast, cheap, and right often enough.

I do not think every IT role disappears. I do think some people will lose their jobs. There is no honest version of this where every help desk technician becomes an architect or security engineer.

**Refusing to use AI will not protect you. It will make you slower than the person who does.**

## What AI Does Not Remove

The systems still have to work.

Users still need access. Mail still has to flow. Backups still have to restore. Someone still has to explain why production is down.

That preserves responsibility. It does not preserve headcount.

High-risk work is not automatically safe from automation either. Restores, firewall changes, account provisioning, and production deployments can all become runbooks. Companies will automate them because mistakes are expensive and consistency matters.

Change control is not protection. If the review process is a rubber stamp today, it will approve an AI-written change tomorrow.

The harder work starts when the runbook is wrong.

The vendor documentation misses the failure. The model invents a parameter that looks real. The tenant has a configuration nobody documented in 2019. The backup job says it succeeded, but the restore does not work.

Someone has to recognize that the expected answer is wrong. Someone has to stop the change, find the actual problem, and own the result.

Physical work will remain too. Cables still fail. Laptops still refuse to image. Remote sites still have equipment nobody documented. A lot of companies already outsource that work. I would not bet my career on being the last person allowed to touch the hardware.

## Six Ways To Stay Valuable As AI Takes The Repeatable Work

### Verify Everything AI Produces

AI is confident when it is wrong.

Read the command. Check the permissions. Open the vendor documentation. Test the rollback. If you cannot verify the answer, you should not put it into production.

Generating the answer is getting cheap. Knowing whether the answer is safe is still valuable.

### Own A System That Matters

Get closer to identity, backups, DNS, firewalls, production, or privileged access. Not because those systems cannot be automated. They can.

The value is knowing how the system should work, how it actually fails, and how to recover it. When the automation breaks, be the person who can tell the difference between a harmless warning and an outage.

Do not confuse ownership with hoarding knowledge. If you are the only person who understands the system, you built another problem.

### Use The Tools Now

Let AI draft the script, the runbook, and the email. Then check the work and remove what does not belong.

Refusing the tool does not make your work more valuable. It makes you slower. Blindly trusting it is not better. Use it for speed, not judgment.

### Learn AI By Using It

You did not learn PowerShell by reading every page of documentation before opening a terminal. You used it. You wrote bad scripts, broke things, fixed them, and learned where the tool helped. You probably learned most vendor tools the same way.

AI is no different. Documentation and prompt guides will only get you so far. Use the models on real work. Ask one to research an error, draft a script, review a change, or explain a system you do not understand.

Pay attention to where it saves time, where it lies, and what context it needs from you. The strategy that works for me may not work for you. You find your angle by using the tool, not reading about it.

### Build Something People Can Reuse

A closed ticket disappears into the queue. A useful runbook, tool, diagram, or postmortem keeps paying off.

Write down the real error message. Document the recovery step that the vendor left out. Build the small tool that prevents the next person from repeating the same ten clicks.

AI may use that documentation later. Good. The point was to stop solving the same problem by hand.

### Delete The Extra Work

AI will happily add a framework to a 40-line script. It will create options nobody asked for, catch errors it should expose, and explain code that should have been deleted.

Your job is not to accept everything it generates. Your job is to know what the problem requires and cut everything else.

That judgment comes from knowing your systems, your constraints, and the cost of getting it wrong.

## Be Concerned, Not Helpless

The threat is real. Some IT jobs will disappear. Some teams will shrink. Pretending otherwise will not help anyone.

Panic is useless too. You still have time to learn the tools, understand where they fail, and move toward work that requires judgment and ownership.

There is hope because this change is not happening without us. Companies still need people who understand the systems, can verify the output, and will take responsibility when the answer is wrong.

Use the tools. Learn how they fit your work. Own the failures. Write down what you learn. Move toward problems where the answer is not already sitting in the ticket template.

Be concerned about what is coming. Then do something useful with that concern.
