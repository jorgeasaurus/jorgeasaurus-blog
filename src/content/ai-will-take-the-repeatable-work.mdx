A lot of IT work is the same ticket again.

Password reset. Add to the group. Rerun last year's script. Follow the screenshots in the wiki. We got good at that. A lot of us built careers on it.

That slice is what a model can already draft. Not "IT." The known, inbound, copy-the-runbook slice. If that is most of your week, the job title is not going to save the mix.

Accountability does not save it either. Someone still signs. That someone can be one remaining senior, a manager who never opens the ticket, an MSP, or a vendor SLA. The signature can stay while the bench leaves.

## Why it might happen

Companies notice cost.

One person with tools can clear an inbound queue that used to take a bench. Password resets and group adds are the obvious ones. A lot of helpdesk and desktop looks like that. Identity, cloud, network, and security have a version of it too, but they are not the same job. The shared part is: request comes in, the fix is known, close it.

I do not think every role disappears. I think a lot of people will find out their week was mostly that queue. Some of those seats just go. There is no honest path that puts every L1 on the change board.

Sitting out the tools does not protect you. The person still named will use them.

## Why it might not

The function does not vanish. Login, mail, backups, and identity still have to work. Someone still gets the ugly page.

That is not the same as headcount staying put. It means the org still needs a name on the outage.

The other trap is treating "scary" as "safe." Restores, firewall changes, joiner-mover-leaver, prod deploys — those can hurt, and they are also runbooks. Shops will automate them *because* the blast radius is high. Change control will not save you if CAB is a rubber stamp. An AI-written RFC sails through the same meeting the human one did.

What is harder to replace is the hour the template does not fit. Vendor docs miss the failure. The model invents a flag that looks official. If you do not catch it, it ships. Local context. This tenant, this week, this leftover from 2019.

The last meter is still a person sometimes. A cable, a rack, a laptop that will not image, a site that never made the wiki. In a lot of SaaS shops that work already belongs to a contractor. Do not build a career on it.

## Five things I would actually do

### Read the output before it ships

Treat the model like a fast junior who does not know when it is wrong. If you cannot check the command, the permission, or the vendor doc, you are just pasting. Speed is cheap. Being able to tell is not.

### Be the person still named

Identity, backups, DNS, firewalls, prod, privileged access — not because they cannot be templated, but because when the template fails somebody still has a name on the outage. Get closer to that. If you cannot, be honest: some seats are the queue, and the queue is getting cheaper.

### Use the tools. Cut what they add.

Let it draft the script, the runbook, the email. You pick the problem and throw away the extra steps. The person who uses the model is easier to keep than the person who will not.

### Leave something behind besides a closed ticket

Friday's incident is gone on Monday. A runbook with the real error string, a small tool, a diagram, a postmortem someone else can follow — that travels with your name. It can also feed the next model. Write it anyway. Tickets empty. A name on work other people reuse does not.

### Delete half of it

Left alone, the model adds a framework to a 40-line fix. The useful part is knowing what not to do *here*, this week. That is local context, not magic taste. You still have to bring it.

I do not always do this.

The known work is going to get cheaper. Accountability will sit on fewer people. Try to be one of them.
